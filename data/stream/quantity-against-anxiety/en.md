Lately, when anxiety hits, I tell myself to do something first.

I've tracked my time on my calendar and with a Pomodoro timer for years.

Looking back at the numbers recently was humbling: I hadn't put in nearly as much time as I thought. Yet I'd spent plenty of it worrying and telling myself I wasn't good enough.

So I've decided to put in more hours before judging whether I'm good enough.

That brought to mind an experiment I read about in Atomic Habits. Jerry Uelsmann, a photography professor at the University of Florida, divided his students into two groups. One would be graded on how many photos they submitted; the other would submit just one photo and be graded on its quality.

The students graded on quantity ended up making the better photos. They had more chances to experiment, make mistakes, and improve. The students graded on quality spent so much time studying theory and chasing the perfect shot that they barely experimented, and their results fell short.

Of course, not everything can be measured, and more hours don't guarantee better results, especially with AI in the picture. Still, I need to do enough work to see what's working and change course when it isn't. The same goes for AI: to learn how to use it well, I have to actually use it.

I came across Manny's take on Google Lab's Vibe Coding game platform:

> An LLM is like a seed of intelligence. Put it at the entrance to a new field, and it can open the door for you.
> Sure, you can open Claude Code or Codex yourself and do just about anything. But if you never start, it's all talk.

This week, I gave myself a simple target: "At least 40 hours on my Pomodoro timer this week, so I can see how much time I actually spent focused." That's eight hours a day, five days a week. If I can't manage eight hours each weekday, I'll have to give up some of my weekend to make up the time.

When I start worrying about everything, I check my Pomodoro timer and calendar: how much work have I actually put in this week? If I haven't even hit my time target, I can keep going instead of getting lost in my thoughts. If I'm worried I won't find a job, I look at how much time I've spent preparing for interviews and applying. If my Japanese doesn't feel good enough, I look at how much I've studied. If I'm worried my writing isn't good or nobody reads it, I look at how much time I've spent writing and getting my work out there.

Time isn't everything, but it's where I have to start. Without putting in the reps, it's too early to worry about quality. Some people really can learn quickly and do a lot in very little time. I'm not one of them, so I'll keep moving forward one step at a time, in my own way. I like the saying, "Focus on your own exam paper. Stop looking around" (source unknown).

I think some of my anxiety and overthinking come from not doing enough and not knowing enough. Taking action gives me a way to look for answers. Each time I find one and get past something that used to scare me, the anxiety eases a little.
